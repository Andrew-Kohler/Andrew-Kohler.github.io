Gameplay Programming

Momentum, Antigravity, and Slopes

Everent is a hoverboarding game where you can ride any surface, no matter the orientation or curvature, because you can rotate your board in any orientation or curvature.

I was responsible for programming those core mechanics, and accounting for all the edge cases that accompany them. I implemented variable force application to ensure the board could stick to concave surfaces, integrated velocity and acceleration into both player ground navigation and air traversal via a designer-accessible system of decay constants, and created a raycast-based gravity solution to allow our Pawn player to stick to surfaces without the advantage of the CMC's tunable gravity.

Character Movement Details

It's Cool To Sweat The Small Stuff

A lot of detail work has gone into the player's locomotion! Using Unreal's physics system to create stable motion has seen me do extenstive research and implementation testing on Unreal 5's latest in physical consistency, experimenting with substepping and async physics tick to create a greater level of determinism.

Some of the finer invisible touches include varying force application at different points on the board based on distance from the ground to allow consistent riding on convex surfaces, and using a cast with a speed-based length to predictively orient towards upcoming concave slopes, I've done a lot of prototyping with different mathematical approaches to hover force as well, experimenting with PID tuning, spring force, and angular force-based approaches to board stabilization.

Bug Fixing and Problem Solving

Experience In Things Going Wrong

A project of this scale doesn't come without bugs! Throughout its lifetime, I've assessed and solved issues relating to both our game logic and usage of the engine.

One of our most pressing gameplay issues had to do with crossing rotation thresholds (moving our board from a rotation of 180 to -180 degrees). The delta rotation was so large that a disproportionate force had been applied compared to how much the board had actually been rotated. I helped to solve this issue by building a debug readout for the board, allowing us to easily enable a suite of values that we could watch for unexpected changes when our player flew off into space.

Recently at the end of the project's lifespan, I've relied not only on debugger stepthroughs and visualizations, but crash and runtime logs to help me solve problems. I've figured out asset corruptions and scene loading errors through them; on a project of this scale, every resource is invaluable.
